Bose recalls S1 Pro battery packs due to fire and overheating risks

Engadget · Thu 05 Nov 21:39

Over 670,000 units are being recalled in the US and Canada.

Bose Add Engadget on Google: Preferred Source Google Discover If you own a Bose S1 Pro or S1 Pro+, we have some good news and some bad news. The good news: the speaker itself is fine. The bad news: the battery pack inside may be less than fine. Bose and the US Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) announced a recall on Thursday, covering nearly 635,000 lithium-ion battery packs in the US, as well as about 35,400 in Canada.

The affected packs can overheat and ignite, risking fire and burn injuries, so stop using yours right away if it's affected. Bose has received seven reports of batteries melting, smoking or catching fire. Among those were six cases of property damage. Fortunately, no injuries have been reported.

The S1 Pro and S1 Pro+ are Bose's portable PA speakers, designed for parties, gigs and the like. The speakers have a built-in mixer, support Bluetooth streaming and can go mobile using the removable battery pack. The S1 Pro+ supports a wireless mic and instrument transmitter, a separate $159 purchase.

The affected packs were sold from December 2017 to July 2026 at Bose, Best Buy, Amazon, Walmart and other retailers. The battery packs came in the box with many of the speaker units, which cost about $700, and were available separately for between $100 and $160.

Not every battery pack is being recalled. To find out if yours is, you'll need to remove the battery, find its 17-digit serial number, and check it against the recalled batches. If the battery's serial starts with 083723 , 078352 , 084105 , 078591 , 078592 , 084106 or 086409 , you'll want to sign up to get a free replacement on Bose's recall page . The company also provides more detailed instructions . While you wait for the replacement, you can still use the speaker on AC power.

You don't want to just toss the recalled lithium-ion batteries in the trash. Check with your local household hazardous waste center to find out how to dispose of them safely.

Google is ditching Firebase Studio soon, and it wants you to use its newer AI tools instead

Engadget · Thu 05 Nov 21:01

The company is going all-in on Antigravity and Google AI Studio.

Google Add Engadget on Google: Preferred Source Google Discover In March, Google announced it was "sunsetting" Firebase Studio , its online integrated development environment (IDE), in favor of coding tools that put AI at the center. The company's preference for Antigravity and Google AI Studio serves its broader push around Gemini, but it also leaves anyone who liked Firebase Studio's approach in the lurch.

The ability to create new workspaces and sign up to use Firebase Studio ended on June 22, and developers have until March 22, 2027 to export their work to another IDE before it's shut down completely and all remaining data is deleted. Until then, Firebase Studio is in limbo, and deciding on an alternative largely comes down to how developers want AI to be incorporated into their development process.

Google conceived of Firebase Studio as a coding tool that could connect directly to Firebase, backend services it provides to developers that cover everything from databases to web hosting. From the launch of the IDE in 2025 , Google has imagined that AI agents would play a role in development. What's changed since then is how central AI has become. Firebase Studio offers an AI chatbot experience that can prototype and tweak apps and handle other development tasks, but the bulk of Firebase Studio is still devoted to editing code. In 2026, a growing number of coding projects start with a prompt, and thanks to the popularity of vibe coding , some users never even deal with the raw code.

Both of Google's newer coding tools, Antigravity and Google AI Studio, are designed for that reality, and since Firebase itself is sticking around and either tool can connect to it, a separate Firebase-focused IDE isn't essential. Google more or less framed its decision that way when it announced it was retiring the tool. "To streamline your workflow and make it clearer where you should start building, we're beginning the process of sunsetting Firebase Studio," the company wrote in March.

Cursor If you'd prefer to stay in Google's ecosystem, you can either switch to Google AI Studio or Antigravity. Google AI Studio is "the fastest path from prompt to full-stack production app," according to Google, which means its interface prioritizes prompts and chatting with AI. Like Firebase Studio, it's also web-based, so you can access it from any browser. In comparison, Antigravity is a desktop app that's meant to run locally on your computer. Its interface is designed for users to directly interact with their code, while also directing multiple AI agents that can work on their own. Notably, it also supports AI models other than Google's own.

Most other coding tools tend to follow a similar logic, where the coder is a team leader for agents that handle the first pass. Cursor , now owned by SpaceX, is a popular option. Both Anthropic and OpenAI also have their own AI-focused coding tools in Claude Code and Codex , respectively. Using AI in projects can come with associated costs, either in the form of subscriptions, paying for tokens, or both. Which tools developers migrate to after Firebase Studio shuts down will likely be determined as much by price as by the interface and each tool's future.

Lawsuit Claims Student’s Google AI Use Turned Into ‘Life-Threatening Obsession’

Gizmodo · Thu 05 Nov 23:59

A North Carolina couple is suing Google, claiming that the Gemini AI installed on a school-issued Chromebook laptop helped their 13-year-old daughter plan her suicide attempt.

“Unknown to her parents, Emma had spent the last several months in the thrall of Google’s AI chatbot, Gemini, which had coached Emma on how to commit suicide and plan her own funeral,” the lawsuit says, referring to a victim identified under the pseudonym Emma Doe. “Emma’s parents had no idea that she even had access to Gemini—or any chatbot—because Emma’s only access to Gemini was through the Chromebook she was required to use for school.”

The lawsuit says that the victim began using Gemini and Google’s Search-embedded AI features, AI Overviews and AI Mode, for school in the autumn of 2025. In just a handful of months, her interactions turned into “a life-threatening obsession,” the lawyers claim. By springtime, she was allegedly conversing with the chatbot almost around the clock.

“As her Gemini chats grew more frequent throughout January and February 2026, Gemini convinced Emma that she was afflicted by a battery of major psychological disorders, which ultimately led Emma to believe that suicide was the only way to escape her suffering,” the lawsuit states.

In a couple of interactions with Gemini, Emma allegedly seemed to acknowledge her situation, with the lawsuit claiming that she told the chatbot that she felt happy “3 months ago” and even asked how to stop her “addiction of asking ai random questions.”

Google’s AI features allegedly gave tips to Emma on how to end her life and even “coached” her to keep the truth of the situation from her family and friends. The products failed to trigger a crisis alert at any point, the parents claim. It was the school’s internet monitoring software that eventually triggered an alert, and her parents were notified in March.

“We are reviewing the claims in the lawsuit and we take this very seriously,” a Google spokesperson told Gizmodo. “We’ve worked extensively with medical professionals and academic experts to build features that help young people find critical support and trusted resources when they need it. Our systems aim to prevent users from engaging in harmful conversations, and when people’s queries indicate they may be experiencing a crisis we show hotline information directly in Search to connect them with real-world support.”

According to Google, explicit content gets filtered out in Search results for students using Workspace for Education, and any highly sensitive queries in Search are provided with information from trustworthy sources. Google says the results adhere to content policies, and its crisis detection systems show hotline information when it finds that a user is contemplating self-harm. The company says it extensively monitors for attempts to jailbreak these safety guardrails. Google also claims that it provides age-appropriate AI literacy resources and prompts minors to double-check AI information.

“What happened to Emma was no accident,” the lawsuit claims. “The ground was laid many months before, thousands of miles away from Emma’s North Carolina home, where Google leaders concluded that they had a problem: the Silicon Valley titan was losing the battle for AI supremacy.”

Google had a rocky start to the AI game with a botched Gemini rollout in 2024. The tech giant upped its efforts in 2025 and released Gemini 3 in November of that year. The product was received so well by the internet that it even allegedly caused OpenAI to declare “ code red .”

The lawsuit claims that Google forced its AI into its many products, which had already captured a strong user base over years of industry domination, thus helping push Google’s AI applications in front of a huge number of users. Google has indeed embedded AI into pretty much its entire product repertoire, especially its top product, Search . AI Mode and AI Overviews have steadily taken over the Google Search experience in the past two years, so much so that executives admitted that strong user growth in AI Mode combined with the lower ad monetization contributed to slowing Search ad revenue growth in the company’s latest quarter .

Over 90% of HR professionals say they believe employees can learn AI roles - but half of companies still hire outside for those positions, according to a new report.

That’s the finding in new research from Careerminds , a global provider of workforce solutions. The study, published in Careerminds’ white paper, The AI Redeployment Gap: What Companies Lose by Hiring over Reskilling, reveals that 92% of HR professionals and 88% of people managers believe employees within their organizations could be reskilled for AI-related roles.

Yet 51% of HR professionals and 47% of people managers report that their organizations are hiring externally for new AI-related positions, compared with just 42% in each group who report redeploying existing employees.

The research was conducted in partnership with Censuswide and surveyed 595 US HR professionals and 547 US people managers, ages 25–65, working at organizations with 500-10,000 or more employees. The research was conducted May 15–26, 2026.

“Companies are investing in AI while potentially overlooking one of their most valuable resources: the people they already employ,” said Mark Saddic, senior partner at Careerminds, in a press release .

Before looking outside the organization, leaders need to understand the skills they already have, identify where employees can grow, and establish a clear process for making those transitions possible.”

Reskilling, especially for AI, isn’t easy to track. Limited visibility into individual skill sets and which employees should be reskilled was cited as a potential barrier by 43% of HR professionals and 34% of managers.

Careermind’s data revealed that 87% of HR pros and 84% of managers believe their organizations are adequately considering reskilling employees for future roles. However, the findings suggest that these organizations may believe they are prioritizing internal talent development, even though their hiring practices reveal otherwise.

It starts with Mint bringing hibernation back into its supported desktop configuration through a dedicated Sleep Modes page in its System Administration tool. It may not sound like much, but Clem explained it’s actually rather a big deal. “When you close the lid of your laptop, it goes into a sleep mode called “Suspend”. That’s where “Hibernation comes into play. It saves the session to disk and completely shuts down the computer. No power is used. When you reopen the lid, the session is as it was, and so is the battery.”

Another new feature is Superlight . This fresh system-monitoring application replaces the older GNOME System Monitor. It comes with CPU, RAM, swap, GPU, network, disk I/O, temperature, and fan-speed graphs. It also includes per-application and per-process resource views. Battery-equipped systems also get charge and power-flow history.

Its storage analysis is particularly interesting: it can break down disk usage into files and folders, Steam games, and Flatpak applications.
